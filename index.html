Object Pronouns

Choose between COD, COI, y, en, ça, and a tonic pronoun by looking at the original complement and the verb that introduces it.

Start here: what should replace the object?

Do not translate an English pronoun mechanically. First identify whether French uses no preposition, à, de, a place phrase, or an explicit “this / that” idea.

Forms at a glance

COD and COI share most first- and second-person forms. The biggest contrast is third person: le / la / les versus lui / leur.

COD Pronouns — direct object pronouns

COD answers “who?” or “what?” directly after the verb, without à or de. The core forms are me / te / le / la / nous / vous / les. Use COD for direct-object verbs such as voir, aider, inviter, regarder, and attendre.

When COD is triggered

Use COD when the person, animal, or thing directly receives the action of the verb.

When not to use COD

Do not use COD just because English says “him,” “her,” “it,” or “them.” French depends on the verb pattern: à + person can point to COI, de + thing often points to en, and a real prepositional phrase usually keeps a tonic pronoun.

COD placement in sentence patterns

The COD pronoun goes before the verb it complements. In passé composé, a preceding COD can trigger written agreement: vu / vue / vus / vues.

COI Pronouns — indirect object pronouns

COI commonly answers “to whom?” with a person. The core forms are me / te / lui / nous / vous / leur. A verb must license the indirect construction; à or English “for” alone does not automatically produce lui / leur. COI leur never takes s.

When COI is triggered

Use COI when the verb naturally points to a person as its indirect recipient, listener, answer target, or beneficiary.

When not to use COI

French depends on the verb pattern and whether the complement is a person, place, thing, direct object, or full prepositional phrase. Keep pour + tonic pronoun unless the verb itself allows a beneficiary COI.

COI placement in sentence patterns

The COI pronoun also goes before the verb it complements, not automatically before the first verb in the sentence.

Adverbial pronoun: y

Y replaces a location other than origin, or à + thing / idea. It goes before the verb it complements and before the auxiliary in passé composé; in an affirmative imperative it follows the verb: J’y vais, J’y suis allé, Vas-y.

Adverbial pronoun: en

En replaces de + thing / idea / origin, a partitive or indefinite phrase, or a quantity phrase. For people, a tonic pronoun is the clearest beginner default, although personal en can occur in real French.

Demonstrative pronoun: ça

Ça means “that / it” and is the everyday spoken form of cela. It usually refers to a nonhuman thing, idea, or situation and keeps that reference explicit: Je pense à ça, Je parle de ça.

Passé composé

Practice le passé composé: helper verb + past participle. Click any verb or sentence form to hear it.

Passé composé quick notes

Most verbs Use avoir in the present tense + past participle: j’ai parlé, tu as fini.
Some movement verbs Use être: elle est allée, nous sommes partis. The past participle agrees with the subject.
Agreement with être Match the subject’s gender and number in writing: il est allé, elle est allée, ils sont allés, elles sont allées.
Usually no subject agreement with avoir Write elle a parlé and elles ont parlé. The past participle does not simply agree with the subject.
Earlier COD can trigger agreement With avoir, a direct object placed before the verb can trigger agreement: les lettres que j’ai écrites, je les ai écrites.
Pronominal nuance Compare elle s’est lavée with elle s’est lavé les mains: a following direct object can block agreement with the reflexive pronoun.

Quick Notes

bien Bien is the adverb: it often means “well,” “fine,” or a judgment like “that’s good.” It does not agree.
bon Bon is an adjective for “good,” especially quality, usefulness, taste, or character: un bon café, une bonne idée.
beau Beau is an adjective for “beautiful / nice-looking,” and appears in weather: Il fait beau.

Amount and degree: beaucoup, un peu, peu, trop, assez

Use these to describe amount, degree, or how much an action happens. Click a word for pronunciation, or click an example sentence for full-sentence audio.

Quantity + noun: use de / d’ after beaucoup, un peu, peu, trop, and assez, even before a plural noun: des livres → beaucoup de livres, trop de livres, assez de livres, peu de livres. They also replace partitive forms: du pain / de la confiture / de l’eau → beaucoup de pain / beaucoup de confiture / beaucoup d’eau. Do not use beaucoup des livres when you simply mean “many books.” For a specific known subset, des can remain because it means de + les: beaucoup des livres que tu m’as prêtés = “many of the books you lent me.” Use un peu de mainly with uncountable or mass nouns; for a few countable items, quelques livres is often more natural.

Tout: three grammar jobs

Start by asking what tout is doing: modifying an adjective, standing before a noun, or replacing a noun phrase. The spelling and pronunciation depend on that job.

1 Adverb Before an adjective: “completely / very”
2 With a noun “whole / all / every”
3 Pronoun Replaces a noun phrase: “everything / all of them”

1. Tout as an adverb: completely / very

When it modifies an adjective, tout normally stays the same. It only agrees before a feminine adjective beginning with a consonant or an aspirated h. Use the number/gender matrix as a quick reference.

Common adverbial chunks

Learn these high-frequency expressions as complete phrases; they do not follow the noun-agreement table below.

2. Tout before a noun: whole / all / every

Here tout agrees with the noun. Singular means “whole / entire”; plural usually means “all,” or “every” with repeated time units. Tout le temps means “all the time”; tous les jours means “every day.” Never write tout les jours.

3. Tout as an indefinite pronoun

Use it to replace a noun phrase. Singular tout means “everything”; there is no standalone feminine singular pronoun *toute. Plural tous and toutes mean “all of them.” As a pronoun, tous is pronounced /tus/, unlike determiner tous les jours /tu/.

Quick Pattern Notes

Informal speech French often places question words at the end: Tu fais quoi ?
Before nouns Quel changes form to match the noun: quel, quelle, quels, quelles.
With vowels Que becomes qu’ before a vowel sound: Qu’est-ce que c’est ?

Yes/no questions Est-ce que starts a yes/no question. N’est-ce pas works like “isn’t it?” or “right?”

Qui Deep Dive

Qui asks about a person. When qui is the subject of the verb, French usually uses the 3rd-person singular verb form: Qui parle ?, Qui veut ?, Qui a ?

Quel Deep Dive

Quel means “which / what” before a noun. It agrees with the noun: masculine/feminine and singular/plural.

Changing sentence order

Special case: quel + être

Use this pattern when quel asks for identity, choice, time, date, address, or a similar “what is / which is” answer.

Special expression: de quelle couleur

Use de quelle couleur to ask what color something is. It often works with être: De quelle couleur est ... ?

Day questions

Use quel jour for day-of-week questions, and simple yes/no questions for days like lundi, mardi, and so on.

Time questions

Use quelle heure to ask “what time is it?” and à quelle heure to ask when something happens.

Que / Quoi Deep Dive

For object questions meaning what, compare the spoken quoi ending, the fronted que form, and the everyday qu’est-ce que pattern.

Special use: que as a connector

Here, que is not a question word. It connects two clauses and usually means that, which English often leaves out. The second clause keeps normal statement word order: Je sais qu’il arrive. Use qu’ before a vowel sound.

Special case: c’est quoi / qu’est-ce que c’est

Use this fixed pattern when asking what is it / what is that.

Comment Deep Dive

Comment asks how something happens or is done. Compare casual spoken order, formal inversion, and the neutral comment est-ce que pattern.

Combien Deep Dive

Combien asks about an amount or price. Before a noun, use combien de + noun; de stays unchanged even before a plural noun.

Y a-t-il Deep Dive

Y a-t-il is the formal question form of il y a, meaning is there / are there. Compare it with casual intonation and the everyday est-ce qu’il y a pattern.

Quick Notes

Lowercase in French Days, months, and seasons are usually lowercase: lundi, janvier, hiver.
Months French commonly uses en for months: en janvier.
Seasons French usually uses en, but spring is very often au printemps.
Relative days Use hier, aujourd’hui, and demain without a preposition: Je pars demain.
Tôt / tard Tôt and tard are adverbs, not adjectives: Elle arrive tôt, Il rentre tard.

Relative time and schedule words

Use these high-frequency words to locate an action around today. Tôt and tard describe when an action happens, so they do not change for gender or number.

An / année, jour / journée, soir / soirée

The short word often marks the countable point or unit. The longer -ée word often emphasizes the lived span, content, or quality of that time.

Seasons
