French Pronouns

Study COD direct object pronouns, COI indirect object pronouns, stressed pronouns, reflexive pronouns, and possessive determiners. Click any form or example to hear it.

COD Pronouns — direct object pronouns

COD answers “who?” or “what?” directly after the verb, without à or de. The core forms are me / te / le / la / nous / vous / les. Use COD for direct-object verbs such as voir, aider, inviter, regarder, and attendre.

When COD is triggered

Use COD when the person, animal, or thing directly receives the action of the verb.

When not to use COD

Do not use COD just because English says “him,” “her,” “it,” or “them.” French depends on the verb pattern: à + person usually points to COI, de + thing often points to en, and real prepositional phrases usually keep tonic pronouns.

COD placement in sentence patterns

These examples show where the COD pronoun moves in normal statements, commands, verb + infinitive patterns, and passé composé. In passé composé, a preceding COD can make the past participle agree: vu / vue / vus / vues.

COI Pronouns — indirect object pronouns

COI answers “to whom?” or “for whom?” with a person. The core forms are me / te / lui / nous / vous / leur. They usually replace à + person, and sometimes a beneficiary meaning like pour + person when the verb allows it. COI leur never takes s; leurs is a possessive determiner, not a COI pronoun.

When COI is triggered

Use COI when the verb naturally points to a person as the indirect recipient, listener, answer target, or beneficiary.

When not to use COI

Do not use lui / leur just because English says “to” or “for.” French depends on the verb pattern and whether the phrase is a person, place, thing, direct object, or real prepositional phrase.

Imperative: être and avoir

The imperative gives instructions, advice, or encouragement. Its three forms correspond to tu, nous, and vous, but those subject pronouns are not spoken: Sois calme. Ayez confiance.

Mini pattern notes

Je becomes j’ before vowel sounds: j’ai, j’habite.

Tu is informal “you.” Vous is formal singular or plural “you.”

On often means everyday spoken “we,” but it still takes singular verb forms: on est, on a.

Ils is “they” for a masculine or mixed group. Elles is “they” for an all-female group.

Passé composé

Practice le passé composé: helper verb + past participle. Click any verb or sentence form to hear it.

Passé composé quick notes

Most verbs Use avoir in the present tense + past participle: j’ai parlé, tu as fini.
Some movement verbs Use être: elle est allée, nous sommes partis. The past participle agrees with the subject.
Agreement with être Match the subject’s gender and number in writing: il est allé, elle est allée, ils sont allés, elles sont allées.
Usually no subject agreement with avoir Write elle a parlé and elles ont parlé. The past participle does not simply agree with the subject.
Earlier COD can trigger agreement With avoir, a direct object placed before the verb can trigger agreement: les lettres que j’ai écrites, je les ai écrites.
Pronominal nuance Compare elle s’est lavée with elle s’est lavé les mains: a following direct object can block agreement with the reflexive pronoun.

-er verbs → -é

-ir verbs → -i

Regular -ir verbs often change to -i: finir → fini, choisir → choisi.

Irregular past participles

Pronominal verbs use être in passé composé: je me suis lavé, elle s’est habillée.

特殊形容词: bon / beau / nouveau / vieux

These common qualitative adjectives usually come before the noun. Bon means good, useful, tasty, or kind; beau means beautiful, handsome, nice-looking, or nice weather. Beau, nouveau, and vieux have a special masculine singular form before a vowel sound: bel, nouvel, vieil. In plural, the indefinite article usually changes from des to de: de bons amis, de beaux vêtements, de nouvelles photos, de vieux livres.

Quick Notes

bien Bien is the adverb: it often means “well,” “fine,” or a judgment like “that’s good.” It does not agree.
bon Bon is an adjective for “good,” especially quality, usefulness, taste, or character: un bon café, une bonne idée.
beau Beau is an adjective for “beautiful / nice-looking,” and appears in weather: Il fait beau.
y Y replaces a place or à + thing/idea: Je vais au parc → J’y vais.

Beaucoup vs un peu vs peu

Use these to describe amount, degree, or how much an action happens. Click a word for pronunciation, or click an example sentence for full-sentence audio.

Quantity + noun: use de / d’ after all three: beaucoup de livres, un peu de lait, peu de temps. This replaces ordinary partitive or indefinite articles. Use un peu de mainly with uncountable or mass nouns; for a few countable items, use quelques livres. For a specific subset, French can keep the definite article: beaucoup des livres que tu m’as prêtés = “many of the books you lent me.”

Adverbial pronouns: y

Y usually replaces à + place or à + thing/idea. It goes before the conjugated verb, before the auxiliary in passé composé, and after affirmative imperatives: J’y vais, J’y suis allé, Vas-y.

L’article partitif: du / de la / de l’ / des

Use partitive articles for an unspecified amount of something, especially food, drink, materials, and abstract nouns. English often says only the noun: Je bois du café. = “I drink coffee.”

Partitive articles in negation and contrast

The common beginner rule is du / de la / de l’ / des → de / d’ after negation, but definite articles, être, contrast, and ne…que need separate treatment.

French Question Words

Quick Pattern Notes

Informal speech French often places question words at the end: Tu fais quoi ?
Before nouns Quel changes form to match the noun: quel, quelle, quels, quelles.
With vowels Que becomes qu’ before a vowel sound: Qu’est-ce que c’est ?
Yes/no questions Est-ce que starts a yes/no question. N’est-ce pas works like “isn’t it?” or “right?”

Qui Deep Dive

Qui asks about a person. When qui is the subject of the verb, French usually uses the 3rd-person singular verb form: Qui parle ?, Qui veut ?, Qui a ?

Quel Deep Dive

Quel means “which / what” before a noun. It agrees with the noun: masculine/feminine and singular/plural.

Changing sentence order

Special case: quel + être

Use this pattern when quel asks for identity, choice, time, date, address, or a similar “what is / which is” answer.

Special expression: de quelle couleur

Use de quelle couleur to ask what color something is. It often works with être: De quelle couleur est ... ?

Day questions

Use quel jour for day-of-week questions, and simple yes/no questions for days like lundi, mardi, and so on.

Time questions

Use quelle heure to ask “what time is it?” and à quelle heure to ask when something happens.

Que / Quoi Deep Dive

For object questions meaning what, compare the spoken quoi ending, the fronted que form, and the everyday qu’est-ce que pattern.

Special case: c’est quoi / qu’est-ce que c’est

Use this fixed pattern when asking what is it / what is that.

Comment Deep Dive

Comment asks how something happens or is done. Compare casual spoken order, formal inversion, and the neutral comment est-ce que pattern.

Combien Deep Dive

Combien asks about an amount or price. Before a noun, use combien de + noun; de stays unchanged even before a plural noun.

Y a-t-il Deep Dive

Y a-t-il is the formal question form of il y a, meaning is there / are there. Compare it with casual intonation and the everyday est-ce qu’il y a pattern.

Quick Notes

Lowercase in French Days, months, and seasons are usually lowercase: lundi, janvier, hiver.
Months French commonly uses en for months: en janvier.
Seasons French usually uses en, but spring is very often au printemps.

An / année, jour / journée, soir / soirée

The short word often marks the countable point or unit. The longer -ée word often emphasizes the lived span, content, or quality of that time.
