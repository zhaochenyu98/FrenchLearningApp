Subject Pronouns + Être / Avoir

Start with the subject pronouns, then study the two most important verbs. Click any card or row to hear the full phrase.

Core subject pronouns

These are the pronouns you combine with verbs like être and avoir.

Être — to be

Use être for identity, state, nationality, location, and descriptions.

Avoir — to have

Use avoir for possession, age, and many common expressions.

Mini pattern notes

Je becomes j’ before vowel sounds: j’ai, j’habite.

Tu is informal “you.” Vous is formal singular or plural “you.”

On often means everyday spoken “we,” but it still takes singular verb forms: on est, on a.

Ils is “they” for a masculine or mixed group. Elles is “they” for an all-female group.

How this tab is organized

Definite le, la, l’, les = “the”
Indefinite un, une, des = “a / an / some”
Partitive du, de la, de l’ = “some”
De + Articles de + le = du, de + les = des; negatives often use de / d’
Demonstrative ce, cet, cette, ces = “this / that / these / those”
Possessive mon/ma/mes, ton/ta/tes, etc.

Definite articles

Use these when you mean the. Choose by gender, number, and whether the next word begins with a vowel sound.

Indefinite articles

Use these for a / an / some with countable nouns.

Partitive articles

Use these for some with things like food, drinks, and other uncountable nouns.

Partitive Article: de + articles

De can mean of / from, and it combines with some definite articles: de + le = du, de + les = des. In many negative sentences, indefinite and partitive articles become de or d’.

Demonstrative determiners

Use these for this / that / these / those. cet appears before a masculine singular noun with a vowel sound.

Possessive determiners

These agree with the noun being possessed, not with the owner. Click any form to hear it with an example.

Vowel-sound tip: before a feminine singular noun starting with a vowel sound, French often uses mon / ton / son: mon amie, ton idée, son école.

French Question Words

Quick Pattern Notes

Informal speech French often places question words at the end: Tu fais quoi ?
Before nouns Quel changes form to match the noun: quel, quelle, quels, quelles.
With vowels Que becomes qu’ before a vowel sound: Qu’est-ce que c’est ?
Yes/no questions Est-ce que starts a yes/no question. N’est-ce pas works like “isn’t it?” or “right?”

Quick Notes

Lowercase in French Days, months, and seasons are usually lowercase: lundi, janvier, hiver.
Months French commonly uses en for months: en janvier.
Seasons French usually uses en, but spring is very often au printemps.
